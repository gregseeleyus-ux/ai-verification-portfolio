# Rumelt's Good Strategy Framework

Purpose: core reference for evaluating and building effective strategy, based on Richard Rumelt's Good Strategy / Bad Strategy.

## Quick reference

Good strategy has:

- A clear diagnosis of the challenge
- A guiding policy to overcome the challenge
- A set of coherent actions to implement the policy

Bad strategy contains:

- Fluff (gibberish masquerading as concepts)
- Failure to face the challenge
- Mistaking goals for strategy
- Bad strategic objectives (impossible, incoherent, or contradictory)

Key test: does this create differential advantage through concentrated application of resources to a pivotal objective?

## The kernel of good strategy

### 1. Diagnosis

Definition (paraphrased from Rumelt, Good Strategy Bad Strategy, 2011): a diagnosis cuts a messy situation down to the few aspects that matter most. It gives the situation a name, connects the facts into a pattern, and points toward where action should focus.

What makes a good diagnosis:

- Identifies the critical challenge or obstacle to progress
- Simplifies complexity without oversimplifying
- Names or classifies the situation in a way that suggests solutions
- Distinguishes symptoms from underlying problems
- Faces reality, especially uncomfortable truths
- Creates insight by linking facts into patterns

What diagnosis is not:

- A list of everything that is wrong
- A description of what you want to achieve
- Goals or aspirations
- Simply restating what everyone already knows without insight

Diagnostic questions:

1. What is preventing progress or causing the current problem?
2. What patterns can we see when we look at the facts?
3. What is the nature of the challenge we face?
4. What would have to be true for this situation to make sense?
5. What are we avoiding facing?
6. If we solved X, would that unlock progress on everything else?
7. What is different about our situation versus similar situations?

Red flags in diagnosis:

- Multiple unconnected problems listed
- No prioritization or identification of root causes
- Describes a desired future state instead of current reality
- Uses generic language that could apply to any situation
- Avoids naming specific obstacles or constraints
- Focuses only on external factors with no internal assessment

Poor diagnosis example: "We face increased competition, changing customer preferences, and need to improve our operational efficiency while growing market share." Why it fails: lists symptoms, offers no insight, does not identify the critical challenge.

Good diagnosis example: "Our premium pricing requires differentiation, but our product has become commoditized because we've failed to maintain technical leadership while competitors copied our features. The critical challenge is that our R&D spending is spread across too many incremental improvements rather than focused on breakthrough capabilities." Why it works: names the core problem, links facts, suggests the domain of action.

### 2. Guiding policy

Definition: a guiding policy outlines an overall approach for overcoming the obstacles highlighted by the diagnosis. It channels action in certain directions without specifying exactly what shall be done.

What makes a good guiding policy:

- Directly addresses the challenge identified in the diagnosis
- Provides clear direction without being overly specific
- Rules out some actions while focusing effort on others
- Creates advantage through how you will compete, not just that you will try harder
- Anticipates major issues and provides a way to approach them
- Leverages sources of advantage or creates new advantages

What guiding policy is not:

- A goal or objective ("increase market share by 20%")
- A list of all possible actions
- Vague aspirations ("achieve excellence")
- Simply "trying harder" at what you already do
- Independent of the diagnosis

Policy development questions:

1. Given our diagnosis, what approach would address the core challenge?
2. What will we do differently, not just better?
3. What is our source of advantage in this approach?
4. What are we choosing NOT to do?
5. How does this policy create coherence in our actions?
6. Does this policy suggest specific types of actions?

Red flags in guiding policy:

- Sounds like it could apply to any company or situation
- No connection to the diagnosis
- Multiple contradictory policies
- Focuses on what to achieve rather than how to overcome obstacles
- Requires resources or capabilities not addressed
- No clear implications for what to do differently

Poor guiding policy example: "We will achieve operational excellence and customer focus while driving innovation and maintaining cost leadership." Why it fails: generic, contradictory, makes no real choices, does not address the obstacle.

Good guiding policy example: "We will abandon our spread of mediocre features and concentrate R&D investment exclusively on advanced analytics capabilities where we have deep expertise, pricing at a premium to customers who need this specific advantage." Why it works: makes choices, addresses the diagnosis, creates direction, leverages advantage.

### 3. Coherent actions

Definition: coherent actions are feasible, coordinated policies, resource commitments, and actions designed to carry out the guiding policy. They are coordinated with one another, building on or reinforcing each other.

What makes actions coherent:

- Each action supports and reinforces the others
- Actions follow logically from the guiding policy
- Resources are concentrated, not scattered
- Actions are feasible given current capabilities and resources
- Sequence and timing create momentum
- Builds on existing strengths or systematically builds new capabilities
- Creates a cumulative effect greater than the sum of the parts

What coherent actions are not:

- A wishlist of everything you would like to do
- Independent initiatives without connection
- Actions that work at cross-purposes
- Steps that assume resources or capabilities you do not have
- Generic best practices without strategic purpose

Action development questions:

1. What specific actions will implement our guiding policy?
2. How does each action reinforce the others?
3. Are these actions feasible with our resources?
4. What is the right sequence to build momentum?
5. Do these actions concentrate our effort or scatter it?
6. What makes these actions different from business as usual?
7. How will these actions overcome the obstacle in our diagnosis?

Red flags in actions:

- Long list of unconnected initiatives
- Each action owned by different groups with no coordination
- Actions that duplicate what competitors already do
- No clear resource allocation or timeline
- Actions that require capabilities not mentioned in the policy
- Contradiction between actions
- No mechanism for coordination or integration

Poor coherent actions example:

1. Improve customer satisfaction
2. Reduce costs by 15%
3. Launch new products
4. Expand into three new markets
5. Upgrade IT systems

Why it fails: generic, unconnected, contradictory resource demands, no concentration.

Good coherent actions example:

1. Shut down development on 8 minor features; triple team size on the analytics engine
2. Hire 3 PhD-level data scientists from a competitor
3. Partner with 2 industry leaders to co-develop advanced use cases
4. Create premium tier pricing at 40% above standard
5. Sales team qualifies only prospects with complex analytics needs
6. Marketing focused exclusively on analytics thought leadership

Why it works: all actions support the same policy, build on each other, and concentrate effort.

## The four hallmarks of bad strategy

### 1. Fluff

Definition (paraphrased from Rumelt): obvious points restated in inflated language and buzzwords so they sound like expertise or insight.

Common fluff phrases:

- "Leverage our core competencies"
- "Synergize across platforms"
- "Maximize stakeholder value"
- "Pursue operational excellence"
- "Drive transformational change"
- "Achieve best-in-class performance"
- "Deliver innovative solutions"
- "Customer-centric approach"
- "Data-driven decision making" (without specifics)
- "Agile and adaptive organization"

How to detect fluff:

- Remove the buzzwords: is there any substance left?
- Could this apply to literally any organization?
- Does it sound impressive but mean nothing?
- Can you explain it in simple terms? If not, it is probably fluff.

The fluff test: replace buzzwords with plain language. If the statement becomes empty or obvious, it was fluff.

### 2. Failure to face the challenge

Definition: bad strategy fails to define the challenge or acknowledge obstacles. It may instead deny problems exist or avoid difficult choices.

Signs:

- The strategy document does not name specific problems
- Focuses only on opportunities, ignoring obstacles
- Assumes challenges will resolve themselves
- Avoids discussing competitive threats
- Describes a desired future without addressing current reality
- Acknowledges the challenge but provides no approach to overcome it
- Pursues goals that are impossible given current obstacles

Why this happens:

- Leadership avoids difficult conversations
- Political pressure to be "positive"
- Fear of acknowledging weakness
- Lack of honest analysis
- Confusing motivation with strategy

Example: "We will become the market leader," without addressing why you are not currently the leader or what obstacle prevents it.

### 3. Mistaking goals for strategy

Definition: stating desired outcomes (goals, vision, objectives) without any plan for overcoming obstacles or creating advantage.

Common pattern: "Our strategy is to [achieve X]" where X is:

- Increase revenue by 30%
- Be the number one provider
- Achieve customer satisfaction of 95%
- Double our market share
- Become a billion-dollar company

Why this fails:

- Goals do not tell you what to do
- No explanation of HOW to achieve them
- No diagnosis of why you do not already have this
- Does not create any advantage
- Ignores obstacles and competition

The goal vs strategy test:

- A goal is a desired outcome
- A strategy is an approach to overcoming obstacles to achieve outcomes
- If it does not explain HOW, it is not strategy

Converting goals to strategy:

- Bad: "Our strategy is to achieve 20% market share."
- Good: "We will achieve 20% market share by focusing exclusively on mid-market customers where incumbents provide poor service, using our faster implementation as the key differentiator."

### 4. Bad strategic objectives

Definition: strategic objectives that fail to address critical issues, are impracticable, or are inconsistent with one another.

A. Impracticable objectives:

- Require resources you do not have and cannot get
- Assume capabilities you do not possess
- Ignore competitive response
- Violate constraints you face

B. Inconsistent or contradictory objectives:

- "Low cost AND premium differentiation"
- "Rapid growth AND perfect quality"
- "Innovation AND risk elimination"
- Multiple priorities that compete for the same resources

C. Objectives that do not address the challenge:

- Failure to connect to the diagnosed problem
- Addressing symptoms rather than causes
- Pursuing what is measurable vs what is important

How to spot bad objectives:

1. Do we have the resources and capabilities to achieve this?
2. Are these objectives in conflict with each other?
3. Do these directly address our diagnosed challenge?
4. Could we achieve these and still fail strategically?

## Sources of strategic leverage

Definition: leverage means focusing competitive energy and resources where they will have the greatest effect.

1. Anticipation: seeing trends or changes before others; preparing while others react; first-mover or fast-follower advantage.
2. Pivot points: identifying where focused effort has disproportionate effect; finding the fulcrum where small force creates large movement; concentrating on the critical factor.
3. Concentration: focusing resources rather than spreading them; overwhelming force at the decisive point; serial focus rather than parallel dilution.
4. Proximate objectives: near-term goals that are feasible; create momentum and learning; build capabilities step by step.
5. Design: how elements fit together and reinforce; system advantage rather than component advantage; coherence creating more than the sum of parts.
6. Focus: doing fewer things better; saying no to good opportunities to pursue great ones; clear priorities that guide resource allocation.
7. Using dynamics: riding waves of change; turning change to advantage; creating second-order effects.
8. Inertia and entropy (in others): exploiting competitors' inability to change; moving while others are frozen; attacking organizational rigidity.

## Strategy evaluation checklist

Kernel completeness:

- [ ] Clear diagnosis that identifies the critical challenge?
- [ ] Guiding policy that provides an approach to overcome the challenge?
- [ ] Set of coherent actions that implement the policy?

Quality tests:

- [ ] Does the diagnosis create insight vs stating the obvious?
- [ ] Does the policy make clear choices about what to do and not do?
- [ ] Do actions reinforce each other and concentrate resources?
- [ ] Is there a source of advantage or leverage?
- [ ] Are actions feasible given resources and capabilities?

Bad strategy tests:

- [ ] Free of fluff and buzzwords?
- [ ] Faces the real challenges directly?
- [ ] Contains actual strategy, not just goals?
- [ ] Objectives are practical and consistent?

Strategic power:

- [ ] Creates competitive advantage?
- [ ] Focuses resources on a pivotal objective?
- [ ] Anticipates or shapes competition?
- [ ] Builds on genuine strengths?
- [ ] Has internal coherence and logic?

## Common strategy patterns

When resources are constrained:
- Diagnosis: limited resources prevent broad competition
- Guiding policy: concentrate force at a narrow, winnable point
- Coherent actions: abandon the periphery, dominate a niche, expand from strength

When facing a stronger competitor:
- Diagnosis: cannot win head-to-head
- Guiding policy: compete where different rules apply or where they have disadvantages
- Coherent actions: asymmetric competition, reframe the game, find their weakness

When the market is changing:
- Diagnosis: current model being disrupted
- Guiding policy: ride the wave of change rather than resist it
- Coherent actions: cannibalize yourself, build new capabilities, anticipate the next phase

When the organization is scattered:
- Diagnosis: energy diffused across too many initiatives
- Guiding policy: ruthless prioritization to concentrate effort
- Coherent actions: kill weak projects, double down on winners, create focus

## Integration notes

With TRIZ:
- Diagnosis identifies contradictions
- Guiding policy often resolves contradictions using TRIZ principles
- Coherent actions implement inventive solutions

With 7 Powers (Hamilton Helmer):
- Diagnosis should identify which Power is relevant
- Guiding policy builds or leverages that Power
- Actions strengthen the Power moat

With the Crux framework (Rumelt, The Crux):
- Diagnosis should lead to identifying the crux
- Guiding policy addresses the crux directly
- Actions turn the crux into opportunity

## Key Rumelt principles

1. Strategy is problem-solving: it starts with diagnosis of a challenge.
2. Strategy requires choice: you must choose what NOT to do.
3. Good strategy is rare: most "strategies" are bad strategy.
4. Advantage comes from asymmetry: being different, not just better.
5. Coherence multiplies power: parts working together exceed the sum of parts.
6. Proximate objectives matter: near-term, achievable goals create momentum.
7. Face reality: you cannot strategize around wishful thinking.
8. Focus beats breadth: concentration of effort at the pivotal point wins.
9. Strategy is not goals: "what" is not "how."
10. Design thinking: how elements fit together creates advantage.

## Quick diagnostic questions

When evaluating a strategy, ask:

1. What is the challenge being addressed?
2. What is the approach to overcome it?
3. What specific actions implement that approach?
4. Do the actions reinforce each other?
5. Is effort concentrated or scattered?
6. What makes this different from obvious or generic approaches?
7. Does this create advantage or just attempt to keep up?
8. Are there clear choices being made?
9. Is this feasible given resources?
10. Could we succeed at this and still fail strategically?
